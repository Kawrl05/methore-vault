# Karl — Research Gap

Existing phishing-awareness research shows that repeated exposure and interactive practice improve detection outcomes, but it does **not isolate gamification specifically** from other active-learning features (repetition, feedback, interactivity).

- [[Gordon et al 2019]]'s multi-year decline in click rates across six US healthcare institutions combined exposure *with* organizational reminders — so the design mixes exposure and institutional reinforcement rather than isolating game mechanics.
- [[Garba et al 2020]]'s drop from 50%+ to 13% click rates came from a university training cycle in Nigeria, but the exact instructional sequence behind that result needs verification against the original source before being characterized further.

Neither study compares a gamified phishing-identification simulation against a **content-, duration-, and exposure-matched traditional condition** under a shared pre-test/post-test design. Because many interventions bundle gamification with narrative, feedback, and simulated consequences all at once, it's hard to know which component actually drives the gains.

Institutions currently choosing between a game-based tool and a seminar/infographic set have no controlled comparative evidence — particularly for graduating students, who face escalating targeted recruitment fraud as they enter the job market (see [[Employment Scams Targeting Graduates]]).
