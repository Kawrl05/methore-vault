# Karl — Justification

Graduating students face targeted recruitment fraud at a documented rate: 40.7% of a surveyed university population encountered suspicious job offers, and 43.2% of those made a payment ([[Anagha et al 2025]]). Attackers now weaponize workflows these students expect — staging fake technical interviews that deliver malware through trusted platforms like GitHub and VS Code ([[Microsoft Defender 2026]]). See [[Employment Scams Targeting Graduates]] for the fuller picture.

If a gamified format produces measurably better detection accuracy than a seminar, institutions gain a concrete, evidence-based reason to choose one training method over the other — rather than defaulting to whichever costs less to run.

**The gap is a matter of bundling.** [[Gordon et al 2019]] and [[Garba et al 2020]] each show repeated exposure works, but neither includes a gamified condition, and neither compares its intervention against an equivalent non-repeated traditional method under the same test. This study isolates training method as the sole variable under a shared measurement design.

**Feasibility.** A between-subjects design — one cohort completes the gamified simulation, a matched cohort attends an equivalent-length seminar or reviews an infographic set — answers all three objectives within an academic term. A shared pool of simulated phishing scenarios, scored identically across conditions, supplies the pre-test/post-test measure. Recruiting graduating students through college networks keeps the sample reachable, and a single testing session per condition keeps the timeline within a term.

**Main design risk:** holding content exposure constant across conditions, since a game and a seminar differ in more than format. Matching both to the same set of red-flag examples controls for this as closely as a single-term design allows.
